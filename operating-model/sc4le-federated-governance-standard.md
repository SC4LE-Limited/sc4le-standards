## SC4LE Federated Governance Standard

The SC4LE Federated Governance Standard defines how organisations make decisions, manage risk, maintain alignment and uphold ethical guardrails within a federated operating model.

It provides a clear, principle-driven structure that balances autonomy with coherence, enabling faster decisions, proportionate escalation and improved flow.

This document is the authoritative definition of the governance that SC4LE implements within organisations.

---

### 1. Purpose

The purpose of the SC4LE Federated Governance Standard is to:

- establish clear, principle-driven decision rights
- reduce decision latency and unnecessary escalation
- ensure ethical, transparent and accountable governance
- enable local autonomy within shared guardrails
- provide consistent governance patterns across domains and value streams
- protect end-to-end flow
- integrate AI-enabled sensing for early insight and risk detection
- support continuous improvement of governance effectiveness
- maintain strategic coherence without centralising control

Federated Governance enables SC4LE to distribute authority while preserving alignment, accountability and ethical integrity.

### 3. Relationship Between SC4LE and the CDA

SC4LE means Strategic Centre 4 Leading Enablement.

SC4LE is the overarching enablement system described by the SC4LE Operating Model.

The Central Design Authority (CDA) is the primary enterprise-level governance capability operating within SC4LE.

The CDA is not synonymous with SC4LE.

Within the Federated Governance Model:

- the CDA maintains enterprise-level and systemic coherence
- Local Design Authorities provide contextual and domain-level governance
- operational teams exercise local authority and create value
- AI-enabled sensing connects operational signals with governance and improvement
- enablement builds sustainable organisational capability

This distinction must remain clear across all governance artefacts and implementations.

### 4. Governing Principles

Federated Governance is grounded in the SC4LE Principles:

1. **Clarity Over Complexity**
2. **Principles Before Processes**
3. **Federated Autonomy**
4. **Sensing Before Steering**
5. **Flow as a First-Class Outcome**
6. **Build–Embed–Withdraw**
7. **Continuous Improvement as Culture**
8. **Ethics & Transparency by Design**

The authoritative definitions of the Principles are maintained in:

`foundations/sc4le-principles.md`

Their governed interpretation is maintained in:

`foundations/principle-interpretation-guide.md`

The Principles are non-negotiable guardrails for governance design and decision-making.

They operate as a mutually reinforcing system.

Every governance component expresses multiple Principles, and every Principle influences multiple governance components.

No governance role or component owns a Principle, and no Principle belongs exclusively to a single governance role or component.

### 5. Governance Architecture

#### 5.1 Overview

SC4LE Federated Governance operates through three interconnected governance levels:

1. **Central Design Authority (CDA)** for enterprise-level and systemic coherence
2. **Local Design Authorities (LDAs)** for contextual and domain-level governance
3. **Operational Teams** for local operational decisions and value delivery

Authority should be exercised as close to the relevant context as practical.

Escalation is an exception mechanism rather than the default route for making decisions.

These governance levels are not intended to create organisational hierarchy or additional management layers.

They define decision authority, accountability and interaction across a federated system.

#### 5.2 Governance Requirements

Federated Governance must be:

- principle-driven
- distributed within clear authority boundaries
- transparent
- proportionate
- evidence-informed
- enabling rather than controlling
- responsive to context
- supportive of end-to-end flow
- subject to ethical and regulatory guardrails
- capable of continuous improvement

Governance mechanisms must not recreate unnecessary centralised approval structures.

### 6. Central Design Authority

#### 6.1 Purpose

The Central Design Authority is the strategic governance capability responsible for maintaining enterprise-level and systemic coherence across the organisation.

The CDA ensures that federated autonomy does not become fragmentation.

#### 6.2 Responsibilities

The CDA:

- stewards the interpretation, application and governance of shared Principles and associated guardrails
- maintains cross-domain and cross-value-stream coherence
- ensures alignment with strategic direction and intended outcomes
- owns enterprise-level and systemic decisions
- maintains the Federated Governance framework
- defines and reviews escalation boundaries
- oversees governance assurance
- integrates insights from AI-enabled sensing
- supports LDAs through guidance, enablement and escalation clarity
- reviews material governance risks and dependencies
- stewards the evolution of governance mechanisms
- maintains alignment with the SC4LE Operating Model Standard

The CDA does not micromanage operational delivery.

The CDA must not unnecessarily reclaim decisions that fall within established LDA or operational-team authority.

#### 6.3 Decision Rights

The CDA owns decisions that:

- affect multiple domains or value streams
- materially change the organisation’s operating model
- introduce or respond to systemic risk
- materially affect regulatory exposure
- establish or change organisation-level guardrails within the approved SC4LE Principles
- have material enterprise-wide consequences
- cannot be resolved within established local authority boundaries
- require a decision across unresolved cross-domain dependencies

Changes to canonical SC4LE Foundation artefacts remain subject to separate Golden Source governance.

#### 6.4 Expected Behaviours

The CDA must demonstrate:

- non-coercive influence
- clarity and transparency
- ethical stewardship
- principle-driven judgement
- proportionate intervention
- systems thinking
- evidence-informed decision-making
- enabling rather than controlling behaviour
- respect for delegated authority

#### 6.5 Anti-Patterns

The CDA must avoid:

- becoming a central approval bottleneck
- micromanaging operational delivery
- reclaiming delegated decisions without material cause
- using governance to control rather than enable
- allowing decisions or rationale to become opaque
- treating conformance as process compliance alone
- over-engineering governance mechanisms
- intervening without relevant evidence or context

### 7. Local Design Authorities

#### 7.1 Purpose

Local Design Authorities are embedded governance capabilities that interpret shared Principles within defined organisational contexts.

LDAs maintain local coherence, support flow and exercise delegated authority within established guardrails.

#### 7.2 Responsibilities

LDAs:

- apply shared Principles to contextual decisions
- exercise delegated decision rights
- support operational teams
- reduce unnecessary escalation
- maintain alignment with shared guardrails
- identify local risks, dependencies and friction
- integrate sensing signals into local governance
- maintain local governance and improvement rhythms
- collaborate with other LDAs where matters cross boundaries
- surface systemic patterns to the CDA
- retain accountability for decisions within their authority

#### 7.3 Decision Rights

LDAs own decisions that:

- affect a defined domain or value stream
- require contextual interpretation
- involve local risk, flow or prioritisation
- remain within established guardrails
- do not exceed agreed escalation thresholds
- can be resolved without material enterprise-wide consequences

#### 7.4 Expected Behaviours

LDAs must demonstrate:

- contextual judgement
- transparency
- ethical awareness
- flow-oriented decision-making
- collaboration with operational teams
- collaboration with other LDAs
- evidence-informed governance
- accountability for local outcomes
- appropriate use of escalation

#### 7.5 Anti-Patterns

LDAs must avoid:

- acting as smaller versions of the CDA
- recreating centralised governance locally
- escalating decisions that remain within local authority
- optimising locally at the expense of wider system flow
- withholding relevant risks or dependencies
- operating outside agreed guardrails
- treating governance as separate from operational work
- using sensing to police teams

---

### 8. Operational Teams

#### 8.1 Purpose

Operational teams are the primary units of value delivery and the first line of day-to-day decision-making.

Operational teams apply shared Principles through normal work and provide the operational signals required for governance and improvement.

#### 8.2 Responsibilities

Operational teams:

- create and deliver value
- make decisions within agreed authority boundaries
- apply shared Principles in practice
- maintain local flow and quality
- identify risks, dependencies and delays
- participate in sensing and feedback loops
- record material decisions where required
- contribute to continuous improvement
- escalate only when an established threshold is reached

#### 8.3 Decision Rights

Operational teams own decisions that:

- affect their immediate work
- are reversible or within agreed risk tolerances
- remain within defined ethical and regulatory guardrails
- do not create material cross-domain consequences
- fall within established team authority
- can be resolved using available context and evidence

#### 8.4 Expected Behaviours

Operational teams must demonstrate:

- ownership
- transparency
- ethical awareness
- attention to flow and quality
- early identification of risk and dependency
- effective use of principles and evidence
- willingness to learn from outcomes
- appropriate collaboration with LDAs

Operational teams are not passive recipients of governance.

They exercise authority within their context and contribute actively to the governance system.

### 9. Decision Pathways

#### 9.1 Purpose

Decision pathways ensure decisions reach the appropriate authority with minimal friction.

They provide clarity about:

- who holds authority
- the scope of that authority
- which guardrails apply
- when collaboration is required
- when escalation is required
- what evidence is proportionate
- how sensing informs decisions
- when decisions and rationale must be recorded

#### 9.2 Decision Types

The Federated Governance Model recognises three broad decision types:

1. **Local decisions** made by operational teams
2. **Contextual decisions** made by LDAs
3. **Enterprise or systemic decisions** made by the CDA

These categories guide decision routing but do not replace contextual judgement.

#### 9.3 Standard Decision Flow

The standard decision flow is:

1. Identify the decision and intended outcome.
2. Determine the applicable authority boundary.
3. Apply the relevant Principles and guardrails.
4. Use available context, evidence and sensing insight.
5. Make the decision at the lowest appropriate authority level.
6. Collaborate where the decision crosses boundaries.
7. Move the matter to the LDA when it exceeds the team’s authority or requires contextual governance.
8. Escalate the unresolved element to the CDA when an approved enterprise or systemic threshold is reached.
9. Record material decisions and rationale where accountability, assurance or learning requires it.
10. Observe outcomes through sensing and feedback.
11. Adapt governance or operating practices where evidence indicates improvement is needed.

#### 9.4 Decision Evidence

The evidence required for a decision must be proportionate to:

- potential impact
- reversibility
- ethical significance
- regulatory exposure
- systemic risk
- cross-domain effect
- uncertainty
- cost or difficulty of reversal

Not every decision requires a formal governance record.

Material, high-impact, irreversible or regulated decisions require sufficient traceability to support accountability and assurance.

### 10. Escalation

#### 10.1 Purpose

Escalation transfers an unresolved element of a decision to an authority with the appropriate scope, context or accountability.

Escalation must not be used to avoid ownership or transfer entire decisions unnecessarily.

#### 10.2 Escalation Thresholds

Escalation should occur when:

- risk exceeds local tolerance
- a decision affects multiple domains or value streams
- ethical guardrails may be compromised
- regulatory exposure materially increases
- a decision creates systemic consequences
- an unresolved cross-domain dependency requires enterprise-level authority
- the relevant authority cannot resolve a material principle tension
- sensing signals indicate an emerging systemic issue
- the decision requires a change to organisation-level guardrails

#### 10.3 Escalation Requirements

An escalation should clearly identify:

- the unresolved matter
- the decision required
- the relevant Principles and guardrails
- the available evidence
- known risks and dependencies
- previous attempts to resolve the matter
- the authority required
- the consequence of delay

The originating authority should retain ownership of all elements that remain within its scope.

#### 10.4 Escalation Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- escalating because a decision is difficult
- escalating reversible or low-risk decisions
- escalating without applying relevant Principles and evidence
- transferring the whole matter when only one element exceeds local authority
- using escalation to seek unnecessary permission
- allowing the CDA to become the default decision-maker
- failing to learn from repeated escalation patterns

### 11. Governance Rhythms

Governance rhythms connect strategic direction, contextual governance, operational delivery, sensing and continuous improvement.

Rhythms must remain lightweight, proportionate and adapted to context.

They define intent rather than mandatory meeting schedules.

#### 11.1 Leadership Cadence

The Leadership Cadence supports:

- strategic alignment
- review of organisational outcomes
- ethical and systemic oversight
- response to material sensing signals
- decisions requiring enterprise-level authority

#### 11.2 Governance Cadence

The Governance Cadence supports:

- CDA and LDA alignment
- review of decision effectiveness
- escalation-pattern analysis
- cross-domain dependency management
- assurance and guardrail review
- governance and operating-model improvement

#### 11.3 Flow Cadence

The Flow Cadence supports:

- bottleneck identification
- review of delays and queues
- dependency analysis
- assessment of work in progress
- rework reduction
- end-to-end flow improvement
- review of cognitive load where relevant

#### 11.4 Sensing Cadence

The Sensing Cadence supports:

- review of leading indicators
- interpretation of risk signals
- assessment of decision latency
- review of governance friction
- identification of emerging systemic patterns
- validation of previous interventions

#### 11.5 Improvement Cadence

The Improvement Cadence supports:

- reflection on governance effectiveness
- review of principle application
- identification of systemic causes
- experimentation and learning
- capability development
- governance and operating-model improvement
- sharing learning across domains

Governance rhythms must not become ceremonial processes disconnected from decisions, learning or outcomes.

---

### 12. AI-Enabled Sensing

#### 12.1 Purpose

AI-enabled sensing provides early insight into governance effectiveness, risk, decision latency, flow and systemic conditions.

Sensing informs human judgement and supports proportionate intervention.

It does not replace human decision ownership or accountability.

#### 12.2 Relevant Signals

Governance sensing may include signals relating to:

- repeated escalation
- decision delay
- governance load
- flow interruption
- rework
- dependency friction
- emerging risk
- ethical or regulatory concerns
- cognitive load
- recurring authority ambiguity

#### 12.3 Sensing Requirements

Sensing must be:

- proportionate
- transparent
- actionable
- relevant to governance outcomes
- integrated into decision and improvement pathways
- subject to appropriate ethical and data guardrails
- interpretable by accountable human decision-makers

#### 12.4 Sensing Guardrails

Sensing must not be used to:

- police individuals
- create unnecessary surveillance
- replace human judgement
- remove human accountability
- automate material governance decisions without appropriate oversight
- overwhelm decision-makers with low-value data
- obscure the basis of recommendations
- measure activity without connection to outcomes

### 13. Assurance Model

#### 13.1 Principle-Driven Assurance

Assurance is based on principle alignment, decision quality, ethical integrity and observable outcomes rather than process compliance alone.

Assurance must confirm that authority is being exercised responsibly without recreating centralised approval structures.

#### 13.2 Assurance Activities

Assurance activities may include:

- review of material decisions
- decision sampling
- ethical guardrail checks
- risk posture review
- dependency analysis
- escalation-pattern review
- interpretation of sensing signals
- review of decision latency
- evaluation of flow and governance friction
- assessment of authority-boundary clarity
- review of corrective and improvement actions

#### 13.3 Assurance Outcomes

Effective assurance should contribute to:

- improved decision quality
- clearer accountability
- reduced governance friction
- reduced unnecessary escalation
- stronger ethical and regulatory alignment
- improved authority-boundary clarity
- earlier identification of systemic risk
- stronger cross-domain coherence
- improved flow
- evidence-informed governance improvement

#### 13.4 Assurance Proportionality

The depth and frequency of assurance must be proportionate to:

- materiality
- systemic risk
- ethical significance
- regulatory exposure
- reversibility
- cross-domain impact
- evidence of recurring governance failure

Low-risk, reversible and local decisions should not be subjected to disproportionate assurance.

---

### 14. Governance Improvement

Federated Governance must evolve through evidence, learning and continuous improvement.

Improvement should focus on:

- decision pathways
- authority boundaries
- escalation thresholds
- governance mechanisms
- assurance practices
- sensing arrangements
- operating rhythms
- organisational capability
- cross-domain collaboration

Governance improvement must:

- address systemic causes rather than only local symptoms
- preserve alignment with the SC4LE Principles
- use evidence from decisions, outcomes and sensing
- avoid unnecessary structural redesign
- maintain clarity and proportionality
- protect delegated authority and end-to-end flow

Changes to Foundation artefacts remain subject to separate Golden Source governance.

### 16. Ownership and Governance

SC4LE owns this standard.

The Central Design Authority stewards the standard on behalf of SC4LE.

The CDA is responsible for:

- reviewing proposed changes
- maintaining governance coherence
- preserving alignment with the Foundations layer
- maintaining consistency with the SC4LE Operating Model Standard
- assessing cross-artefact impact
- maintaining terminology and conceptual consistency
- reviewing material changes to supporting governance artefacts
- stewarding the evolution of the Federated Governance Model

Changes to this standard must follow the repository’s applicable discovery, impact-assessment, contribution and review requirements.

Updates must not introduce competing governance models, alternative terminology or local implementation preferences as canonical SC4LE doctrine.

### 17. Conformance

An organisational Federated Governance implementation conforms to this standard when it:

- applies the approved SC4LE Principles
- distinguishes SC4LE from the CDA
- establishes appropriate CDA, LDA and operational-team decision rights
- distributes authority within clear guardrails
- exercises authority as close to relevant context as practical
- treats escalation as an exception
- transfers only unresolved decision elements during escalation
- maintains visible human accountability
- protects end-to-end flow
- integrates appropriate sensing and feedback
- provides proportionate assurance
- supports continuous governance improvement
- remains aligned with the approved Foundations layer and Operating Model Standard

Conformance does not require identical organisational structures, role titles, meeting schedules or tooling.

SC4LE Federated Governance is applied contextually within its approved Principles, guardrails and architectural requirements.
